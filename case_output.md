
## Situation-Complication-Question

**Situation:**
A hospital network is evaluating wearable patient monitoring devices to reduce unplanned readmissions and associated penalties.

**Complication:**
The upfront investment ($480K) is substantial and payback depends on consistent delivery of readmission reductions. There are questions about clinical adoption, technical integration with legacy EHR systems, and whether projected savings will materialize at full scale.

**Question:**
Should the hospital network proceed with the $480K wearable monitoring investment given the expected $290K annual benefit?

## Issue Tree

**Four Key Decision Drivers:**

1. **Technical Feasibility**
   - Can wearables integrate with existing EHR and data infrastructure?
   - Will real-time data feeds be reliable and actionable?
   - Device durability and battery life for patient compliance?

2. **Financial Case**
   - Payback period: 19.9 months (under 2 years)
   - NPV over 5 years assuming consistent benefit
   - Hidden costs: support staff, device replacement, software licensing

3. **Implementation Risk**
   - Clinical staff adoption and training burden
   - Patient compliance and engagement rates
   - Data security and privacy compliance (HIPAA)

4. **Organizational Readiness**
   - Change management capacity and competing priorities
   - Governance: who owns the monitoring program?
   - Scalability across multiple hospital locations

## Financial Case

**Investment Summary:**
- Upfront Cost: $480,000
- Annual Benefit: $290,000 (from avoided readmission penalties)
- **Payback Period: 19.9 months (~20 months)**

**Financial Metrics:**
- Year 1 net position: -$190K (8 months until breakeven)
- Year 2 net position: +$100K (cumulative)
- Assuming consistent $290K annual benefit thereafter

**Key Assumption:**
The financial case hinges entirely on achieving and sustaining the projected $290K readmission savings. Any shortfall in clinical outcomes or penalties reduction directly reduces ROI. A 20-month payback is attractive for healthcare IT, but assumes no major device replacement costs or training overhead in year 1.

## Recommendation

**Recommendation: PROCEED, with three conditions**

**Supporting Reasons:**
1. **Strong financial return**: 19.9-month payback is well within typical healthcare IT thresholds; positive cash flow achieved in year 2 with sustained benefit thereafter.
2. **Alignment with industry trends**: Readmission penalties are growing; proactive monitoring is increasingly table-stakes for network competitiveness.
3. **Scalability optionality**: Pilot in one facility first to validate savings and refine operations before network-wide rollout.

**Biggest Risk: Savings Realization Failure**
Clinical outcomes may not match projections due to poor clinician adoption, patient non-compliance, or fragmented care workflows. A 10-15% shortfall in readmission reduction would extend payback to 24+ months and erode the business case.

**Mitigation:**
- Conduct a controlled 6-month pilot at one high-readmission unit with clear KPIs (readmission rate reduction, device compliance, staff engagement)
- Assign a clinical champion to drive adoption
- Build in a go/no-go decision gate at month 6 before full network deployment
- Structure vendor contract with clawback provisions if promised readmission reductions are not met

- **Total Cost of Ownership risk:** Does $290K account for device replacements, software licensing, IT support, and training annually? If not, net benefit may be lower.

**Sensitivity Consideration:** A 10% reduction in annual benefit drops payback to 21.9 months; a 20% reduction extends payback to 24.9 months—still acceptable but closer to breakeven risk.

## Recommendation

**Recommendation: PROCEED** with a phased pilot approach.

**Supporting Reasons:**

1. **Favorable Financial Timeline:** The 19.9-month payback period is well within healthcare capital investment norms (typically 2–3 years), and provides positive cash flow from year 2 onward. This is financially prudent.

2. **Regulatory and Market Alignment:** Value-based care penalties are structural and likely to persist; wearable monitoring directly mitigates this known cost. The device addresses a core business pain point rather than a speculative growth opportunity.

3. **Operational Co-Benefits:** Beyond penalty reduction, wearables improve patient engagement, enable early clinical alerts, and generate data for care optimization. These secondary benefits strengthen the business case.

**Biggest Risk: Readmission Benefit Realization**
The $290K annual saving assumes that deployed wearables meaningfully reduce readmissions. In practice, devices alone do not prevent readmissions—clinical staff must respond to alerts, and patients must comply with recommendations.

**Mitigation Strategy:**
- Launch a **6-month pilot** with 2–3 high-risk patient cohorts (e.g., heart failure, COPD) to validate the readmission reduction rate before full deployment.
- Bundle the device rollout with **care coordination process redesign** and clinician training to ensure alerts trigger timely interventions.
- Define a **success threshold** (e.g., 15% readmission reduction required to proceed to scale); if pilot results are weaker, re-evaluate business case and vendor/device alternatives.
